eSIMs Explained Like You're 5: The Complete Guide for Travellers (2026)

What is an eSIM, how does it work, and how do you set one up before your next trip? A plain-English, step-by-step guide - no jargon, no assumptions.

Arpit Kumar
Co-founder
·
July 27, 2026·6 min read

TL;DR

  • •An eSIM is a digital SIM - same networks, same signal, no plastic.
  • •Setup takes about 3 minutes at home, before you fly.
  • •A week of travel data typically costs $5–9 vs $10–15 per day for roaming.
  • •Your home number stays active for OTPs and WhatsApp the whole time.

You're at the airport. Your flight lands in six hours in a country where your phone plan doesn't work. You have three options: pay your carrier roaming charges that could buy a nice dinner every single day, hunt down a SIM card kiosk after landing and hand over your passport to a stranger, or do something that takes three minutes from your couch right now.

That third option is an eSIM. And if you've heard the word but never quite understood it, this guide is for you. By the end, you'll know exactly what an eSIM is, how it works, and how to set one up yourself - no tech background required.

First: what does a SIM card actually do?

That little plastic chip in your phone does one job: it proves to a mobile network who you are, so the network agrees to give you calls, texts, and data. Think of it as a membership card. The network sees the card, recognises the membership, and lets you in.

That's it. That's the whole job. Which raises an obvious question: why does that need to be a physical piece of plastic?

An eSIM is the same thing, minus the plastic

eSIM stands for "embedded SIM." Instead of a removable chip, your phone has a tiny built-in chip that can download the membership card digitally. Same job, same networks, same signal - the only difference is how it gets into your phone. A physical SIM arrives by mail or across a shop counter. An eSIM arrives by QR code, usually in under three minutes.

Nothing about the actual connection changes. Your phone talks to the same cell towers with the same quality. If a physical SIM is a paper ticket, an eSIM is the ticket in your phone's wallet app. The train doesn't care which one you carry.

Why travellers specifically should care

Three reasons, in order of pain:

1. Money. International roaming from your home carrier commonly runs $10–15 per day for a usable data pass - and pay-per-use rates without a pass can hit several dollars per megabyte. A local eSIM for the same country is frequently $5–9 for an entire week. Over a two-week trip, that's the difference between pocket change and a $150+ line item on your bill.

2. Time. With a physical SIM abroad, you land tired, find a kiosk, queue, hand over your passport, wait for someone to photocopy it, and fiddle with a paperclip. With an eSIM, you install it at home before you fly and turn it on when the wheels touch down. You have Google Maps before you clear immigration.

3. Your own number stays alive. This is the part almost nobody tells you: modern phones run two SIMs at once. Your home SIM stays in the phone receiving OTPs, bank alerts, and WhatsApp messages, while the eSIM handles data. You don't go dark at home just because you're abroad.

Does your phone support eSIM?

Almost certainly yes, if it was made in the last five or six years. iPhone XS/XR (2018) and newer, Google Pixel 3 and newer, most Samsung Galaxy S20+ era flagships and newer. Ten-second check:

  • Dial *#06# to see a list of Device informations, having EID in the list means your device is eSIM compatible.
  • iPhone: Settings → General → About → scroll down. If you see "Available SIM" or "Digital SIM," you're set.
  • Android: Settings → search "eSIM" or "SIM manager."

One caveat: phones bought on carrier instalment plans are sometimes carrier-locked. A locked phone can't use another network's eSIM until it's unlocked - usually a free request once the phone is paid off.

How to set up your first eSIM, step by step

  1. Buy a plan for your destination from an eSIM provider's app or website. You'll pick a country (or region), an amount of data, and a duration.
  2. Receive a QR code - instantly, by email or in the app.
  3. Install it: Settings → Mobile/Cellular → Add eSIM → scan the QR code. Your phone downloads the profile in about a minute. Do this at home on WiFi, before you travel.
  4. Label it something like "Japan trip" so your SIM list stays sane.
  5. Configure two switches: set your data to the travel eSIM, and leave your home SIM active for calls/texts if you want to stay reachable. Turn OFF data roaming on your home SIM specifically - that's what protects you from surprise charges.
  6. On landing: toggle the travel eSIM on (if you hadn't already). Within a minute or two you'll see bars. That's it.

The first time takes five minutes because you're reading instructions. Every time after that, it takes ninety seconds.

The seven mistakes first-timers make

  1. Installing the eSIM on hotel WiFi instead of at home. Install before you fly; activation abroad then takes seconds.
  2. Deleting the eSIM instead of turning it off. Most eSIM QR codes are single-use. Deleting the profile mid-trip can mean buying a new plan. Toggle off, don't delete.
  3. Leaving data roaming ON for the home SIM. This is how people use an eSIM and still get a roaming bill. Home SIM: roaming off. Travel eSIM: roaming on (for the travel eSIM, "roaming" is normal - it's how regional plans work).
  4. Buying single-country plans for multi-country trips. Regional plans exist - one eSIM can cover a whole continent or a custom set of countries.
  5. Assuming the eSIM gives you a phone number. Most travel eSIMs are data-only. Calls happen over WhatsApp/FaceTime - which is how you were going to call anyway.
  6. Not checking the phone is unlocked. Sixty seconds of checking beats discovering it at 30,000 feet. A locked phone restricts network access to a single original carrier.
  7. Waiting until the trip to learn all this. You just fixed that one.

The honest downsides

eSIMs are not magic. Data-only plans mean no local number (a problem only if you need to receive local SMS, e.g., some local delivery apps). Very old or budget phones may lack support. And if you swap phones mid-trip, moving an eSIM is more fiddly than moving a plastic chip. For 95% of travellers, none of these outweigh the savings and convenience.
